Income Tax Guide: How Your 2025 Pay Is Actually Taxed

The most common tax misunderstanding is also the simplest: your whole income is not taxed at your top rate. US federal tax works in brackets — each slice of income is taxed at its own rate. Once you see that, a pay rise stops looking like a trap, and your payslip starts making sense. Here is the whole thing, worked out on one real example.

This guide is general information only, not tax advice. State tax, deductions, and credits change your real result.

1. Worked example: $80,000, single filer, 2025

  • Gross income: $80,000
  • Standard deduction (single, 2025): $15,750
  • Taxable income: $80,000 − $15,750 = $64,250
  • Federal tax, by bracket: 10% on the first $11,925 = $1,192.50; 12% on the next $36,550 = $4,386.00; 22% on the remaining $15,775 = $3,470.50
  • Total federal tax: $9,049.00
  • Social Security (6.2%): $4,960.00
  • Medicare (1.45%): $1,160.00
  • Total tax in this example: $15,169.00
  • Take-home pay: $80,000 − $15,169 = $64,831.00 (effective rate about 19.0%)
The key point: Only the last $15,775 is taxed at 22%. The first slices are taxed at 10% and 12%. That is why the effective rate (19.0% here, including Social Security and Medicare) is lower than the top bracket rate — and why moving into a higher bracket never makes a pay rise worth less overall.

2. Marginal rate vs effective rate

Your marginal rate is the rate on your next dollar — 22% in this example. Your effective rate is total tax divided by gross income — 19.0% here. When someone says “I am in the 22% bracket,” they mean marginal. When you budget, use take-home pay, which reflects the effective rate.

3. What this example leaves out

State and local tax, retirement contributions, health insurance premiums, and tax credits (such as child tax credits) are not in these numbers. A 401(k) contribution, for example, usually lowers taxable income in the year you make it. Run your own numbers in the calculator with your filing status and deductions for a closer estimate.

Frequently asked questions

Does a pay rise ever leave me with less money?

Not from federal brackets alone. Only the income above each threshold is taxed at the higher rate, so more gross pay always means more take-home pay, even if the marginal rate rises.

Why is my payslip different from this example?

Because of state tax, benefits, retirement savings, and withholding choices. This example is federal tax plus Social Security and Medicare only, for a single filer taking the standard deduction.

Is this tax advice?

No. This is general information and a worked example only. For your own return, use a qualified tax professional or the IRS guidance for your situation.
