Investment Growth Guide: How Time Beats Timing

Ask most people what makes an investment grow, and they will say picking the right stock at the right time. In practice, for ordinary savers, two far less exciting things matter more: how much you put in regularly, and how long you leave it alone. Compounding — earning returns on your earlier returns — does the heavy lifting, and it needs time more than it needs brilliance. Here is one example that shows exactly how much.

This guide is general information only, not investment advice. Real returns vary, markets fall as well as rise, and past growth does not guarantee future results.

1. Worked example: $10,000 plus $200 a month at 7%

  • Starting amount: $10,000
  • Monthly contribution: $200 for 20 years (240 months)
  • Assumed return: 7% per year, compounded monthly
  • Total deposited: $10,000 + (240 × $200) = $58,000
  • Value after 20 years: $144,572.72
  • Growth (not deposits): $144,572.72 − $58,000 = $86,572.72
The striking part: More than half of the final value — $86,572.72 out of $144,572.72 — came from growth, not from money paid in. And most of that growth arrives in the later years, when the balance is largest. That is why starting early beats starting big, late.

2. A smaller, shorter example for comparison

Start with $5,000, add $100 a month, same 7%, for 10 years: the result is about $27,356.79, from $17,000 deposited. The growth is about $10,356.79. Double the time and double the monthly amount, as in the first example, does far more than double the result — that is compounding at work, and it is also why the first 10 years look disappointingly slow.

3. Three honest warnings

7% is an assumption, not a promise. It is a commonly used long-run illustration for diversified stock investing, before inflation, tax, and fees. Your actual return will differ, year to year and overall.

Inflation eats part of the gain. $144,572 in 20 years will not buy what $144,572 buys today. Compare growth to inflation, not to zero.

Regular beats perfect. Missing months, stopping during falls, or waiting years for the “right moment” usually costs more than a slightly imperfect investment choice. Automate the contribution if you can, then leave it alone.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a large starting amount?

No. In the first example, the monthly $200 contributed $48,000 over 20 years — nearly five times the $10,000 starting amount. Regular contributions matter more than a big start.

What if the market falls?

It will, at some point. In a long plan, falls while you are still contributing mean your monthly amount buys more. That is uncomfortable, and it is also how long-term compounding recovers — but it is not guaranteed, and no calculator can promise it.

Is this investment advice?

No. This is general information and a worked example only. For personal investing decisions, consider a qualified financial adviser.
