Loan Calculator Guide: How Your Monthly Payment Is Worked Out

When a lender quotes you a loan, the number that matters most is not the loan amount — it is the monthly payment, and how much interest you will pay on top of it. Most people only look at the first number and get surprised by the second. This guide shows you, with one worked example you can check by hand, exactly how both are calculated.

This guide is general information only, not personal financial advice. Loan terms, fees, and your credit situation change the real offer you get.

1. The formula, in plain words

For a standard fixed-rate loan with equal monthly payments, the payment depends on three things: how much you borrow, the monthly interest rate (the yearly rate divided by 12), and how many months you take to repay it. Early payments are mostly interest, because the balance is at its highest. As the balance falls, more of each payment goes to the principal.

That is why a longer loan feels cheaper month to month but costs far more in total — you are paying interest for more months, on a balance that falls more slowly.

2. Worked example: $20,000 at 6% for 5 years

  • Amount borrowed: $20,000
  • Yearly rate: 6%, so the monthly rate is 6% ÷ 12 = 0.5% (0.005)
  • Term: 5 years = 60 monthly payments
  • Monthly payment: $386.66
  • Total paid over 60 months: 60 × $386.66 = $23,199.36
  • Total interest: $23,199.36 − $20,000 = $3,199.36
Quick check: $386.66 a month sounds small, but over 5 years it adds $3,199.36 — about 16% on top of what you borrowed. Always compare total interest, not just the monthly payment, when you compare two loan offers.

3. What happens if you pay a little extra?

On that same $20,000 loan, adding just $50 a month (paying $436.66 instead of $386.66) finishes the loan in about 53 months instead of 60, and total interest falls to about $2,770.30. That is roughly $429 saved and 7 months sooner debt-free, from $50 a month.

Small extra payments work so well because they cut the balance directly, which cuts every future interest charge. Before you do this, check that your lender does not charge a prepayment penalty — most do not, but it is worth one question.

4. Three mistakes people make with loans

Choosing only by the monthly payment. A 7-year loan will always look cheaper per month than a 5-year loan on the same amount. Check the total interest before you sign.

Ignoring fees. Origination fees, late fees, and add-ons are not in the simple payment formula. Ask for the total cost, in dollars, not just the rate.

Borrowing for the payment, not the purchase. If a payment only fits when the term is stretched to its maximum, the purchase may be too expensive for your budget right now. Run the numbers on a shorter term first.

Frequently asked questions

Why is my first payment mostly interest?

Because interest is charged on the full balance at the start. On the $20,000 example, the first month's interest alone is $20,000 × 0.5% = $100, so only $286.66 of the first $386.66 payment reduces the balance. The split improves every month as the balance falls.

Is a lower rate or a shorter term more important?

Both help, but a shorter term usually saves more interest than a small rate cut, because you stop paying interest sooner. Try both changes in the calculator and compare total interest, not just the payment.

Is this personal financial advice?

No. This is general information and a worked example only. Your rate, fees, and the right loan for you depend on your credit, income, and the lender's terms.
