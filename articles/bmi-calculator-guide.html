BMI Calculator Guide: What Your Number Really Means

BMI — body mass index — is one of the most quoted health numbers, and one of the most misunderstood. It is not a measure of body fat, fitness, or health on its own. It is simply a ratio of weight to height, used as a quick screening tool. This guide shows you how it is worked out, what the categories mean, and where it falls short, with one example you can check by hand.

This guide is general information only, not medical advice. Talk to a clinician about what your number means for you.

1. The formula

With pounds and inches, the formula is: BMI = 703 × weight in pounds ÷ (height in inches × height in inches). The 703 is just a conversion number that makes the imperial result match the metric formula.

2. Worked example: 5'9", 180 lb

  • Height: 5 feet 9 inches = 69 inches
  • Weight: 180 lb
  • BMI = 703 × 180 ÷ (69 × 69) = 126,540 ÷ 4,761 = 26.6
  • Category: Overweight (25.0–29.9)
  • Healthy weight range for 69 inches (BMI 18.5–24.9): about 125.3 to 168.6 lb
Quick check: At 180 lb, this person is about 11 lb above the top of the healthy range for their height (180 − 168.6 = 11.4 lb). That is a useful target to discuss — not a verdict on their health.

3. The CDC categories

  • Below 18.5: Underweight
  • 18.5–24.9: Healthy weight
  • 25.0–29.9: Overweight
  • 30.0 and above: Obesity

4. What BMI does not tell you

Two people with the same BMI can be very different. Muscle weighs more than fat, so a muscular person can land in the overweight range while carrying little body fat. Age, sex, and where fat is carried also matter, and BMI says nothing about blood pressure, cholesterol, fitness, or diet. Use it as a starting point for a conversation, never as a final judgement.

Frequently asked questions

Is BMI accurate for athletes?

Often not. Athletes with a lot of muscle frequently score as overweight or obese on BMI alone. Waist measurement and body composition give a fuller picture.

How much do I need to lose to reach the healthy range?

Work it out from your own height, not someone else's target. For the example above, the gap is about 11 lb — a realistic, gradual goal rather than a crash target.

Is this medical advice?

No. This is general information and a worked example only. For personal health decisions, speak to a qualified clinician.
